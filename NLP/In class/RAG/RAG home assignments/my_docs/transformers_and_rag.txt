Transformers, Embeddings, and Retrieval-Augmented Generation

The transformer architecture, introduced in 2017, changed natural language processing. Its central
idea is the self-attention mechanism, which lets the model weigh the importance of every word in a
sequence relative to every other word. Unlike older recurrent networks, transformers process all
tokens in parallel, which makes them fast to train on large datasets.

Transformers are the foundation of large language models such as GPT and BERT. These models are first
pre-trained on huge text corpora and then fine-tuned for specific tasks like translation,
summarisation, or question answering.

A key building block for search is the embedding. An embedding model turns a piece of text into a
vector of numbers so that texts with similar meaning end up close together in vector space. Models
like all-MiniLM-L6-v2 produce 384-dimensional embeddings and are widely used for semantic search.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation, or RAG, combines retrieval with generation. Instead of relying only
on what a language model memorised during training, a RAG system first retrieves relevant documents
from a knowledge base using embeddings, and then passes those documents to the model as context. This
helps the model give accurate, up-to-date answers and reduces hallucination, because the answer is
grounded in real retrieved text.

A typical RAG pipeline has four steps: split documents into chunks, embed the chunks and store them,
retrieve the most similar chunks for a user query, and finally generate an answer using the retrieved
chunks as context. Choosing a good chunk size and the right number of retrieved chunks, often called
top_k, has a large effect on the quality of the final answer.
